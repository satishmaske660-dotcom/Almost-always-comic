Chapter One

The first steps of an unspoken love.

The Commute

Every single evening at 6:42, Dadar Station turned into a storm of running feet, loud shouts, and tired faces. Hundreds of people pushed and shoved on Platform 3, just trying to make it home after a long day. But in the middle of all that noise and rushing crowd, Rohan never moved. He always stood under the exact same rusted ceiling fan, waiting by the old iron pillar. His hand held his bag strap tightly, right at the spot where Tara’s headphone wire always used to get caught. He didn’t need to look at the big clock board. He knew her college schedule by heart. He knew which train she caught when she was exhausted, and he knew how many steps she took to walk down the crowded foot overbridge.

"You’re still standing by that same pillar, Ro," a familiar voice said right behind him.

"It’s the only spot where people don’t knock your bag off,"

Rohan replied with an easy, quiet smile, turning around to face her.

"You’re a terrible liar,"

Tara laughed softly. She held up a small paper cup of warm chai.

"You stand here because you know I get lost if you move even five feet away."

She was holding her black drawing tube under one arm. Her hair was loose and slightly messy from the afternoon wind, and her silver earrings swayed gently against her neck. She looked so tired, but the moment she saw him, her shoulders relaxed completely, as if she had finally reached a safe shore. Without saying a word, Rohan reached over and took the heavy plastic drawing tube from her hand, slipping it onto his own shoulder. It was something he had done every day for four years. She never had to ask for help, and he never made her feel like helping was a burden.

The Shield in the Crowd

Far down the dark tracks, the yellow headlights of the local train appeared like two bright eyes. The platform suddenly woke up. People picked up their heavy bags and pushed forward, eager to grab any spot by the train doors. The air smelled of rain, damp clothes, and old railway dust.

"The professor completely tore my project apart today, Ro,"

Tara said, looking down into her half-empty tea. Her voice was unusually quiet.

"He told me my library design was too simple. He said I waste time trying to make buildings feel warm, when all the city cares about is glass and steel."

"He only understands numbers, Tara,"

Rohan told her gently.

"He doesn’t understand that people want to feel safe when they enter a room. They don’t remember how big a building is. They remember how the afternoon sunlight falls across the floor."

"Do you really mean that? Or are you just trying to cheer me up?"

"I’ve seen every single line you’ve drawn since our first semester," he answered, looking straight into her eyes.

"You put your heart into your work. If he can’t feel that, it’s his mistake, not yours."

Tara looked up, and the deep sadness on her face slowly melted away. She didn’t make a joke or look away. She just reached out and gently touched his sleeve with her fingertips. That simple, warm touch meant everything to him, even though he knew she only meant it as gratitude between two close friends. Then, the train came screaming to a halt. The crowd rushed the doors like an angry wave. Rohan moved without thinking. He stepped in front of her, wedging his broad left shoulder hard against the steel doorframe. He made his body into a wall, taking all the hard shoves and elbows from the crowd so not a single person would push into her.

"Grab my jacket and step in," he called over the screech of the brakes.

"I’m holding on, Ro," she whispered, her fingers clutching his jacket with total, unquestioned trust. He guided her inside the compartment safely, straight to the open doorway where the cool evening breeze could touch their faces and give them room to breathe.

The Shared Earphone

As the train picked up speed past Matunga, the heavy, humid air of the platform turned into cool, fresh wind. Down below the railway bridges, the dark water of Mahim creek rushed past, catching the orange city streetlights like spilled paint. Everyone around them was quiet and exhausted, resting against the metal walls as the train swayed back and forth on the tracks. Tara reached into her small pocket and pulled out her white wired earphones. She untangled them patiently, put the right one into her own ear, and handed the other side to Rohan. She didn’t even have to ask. Sharing this wire was a habit they had kept on this same train for hundreds of evenings.

"True care doesn’t need big promises. It lives inside simple habits—sharing an earphone, standing beside someone in the crowd, and staying close through the dark ride home."

A gentle acoustic song filled the earbud, drowning out the loud metal noise of the train wheels. Rohan leaned his shoulder against the doorframe, watching the cool sea breeze move the loose hair around her forehead. She leaned against the pillar right next to him, her arm resting gently against his jacket.

"Do you ever wonder what happens after college, Ro?" she asked suddenly, her voice quiet against the wind.

"What do you mean?" he asked, turning his head slightly toward her.

"When we become real architects. When we have our own separate jobs, and we don’t take the 6:42 local together anymore. What if we get too busy for this?"

"I don’t think either of us will ever forget this train, Tara," he murmured.

"Promise me something, then," she said, looking right at him with deep, serious eyes.

"Promise that no matter how big life gets, we won’t become those strangers who only message each other once a year on birthdays."

Her words felt like a quiet ache in his chest. She was asking for an eternity of friendship, completely unaware that he loved her with every piece of his heart. But he didn’t let his eyes waver. He swallowed his unspoken pain, smiled softly, and gave her the only answer that mattered.

"I promise," he whispered into the rush of wind.

"I’m never going anywhere."

Chapter Two

Old habits, quiet comfort, and a difficult question.

Sunday Market & Old Habits

Sunday morning in South Bombay had a quiet, peaceful charm that the rest of the busy week could never match. The loud office traffic was gone, leaving the wide stone pavements around Flora Fountain empty and calm. Second-hand book sellers had laid out big blue sheets on the pavement, stacking hundreds of vintage books that smelled of sun, old glue, and paper. Tara had already spent nearly an hour kneeling beside a pile of old history folios, her fingers covered in light gray dust. Rohan stood just a step behind her, carrying her cotton tote bag, which was already heavy with thick paperbacks she couldn’t bear to leave behind.

"Ro, look! You have to see this!" she cried out, holding up a huge, battered book with real excitement on her face.

"Careful, the binding looks ready to snap,"

Rohan warned softly, crouching down on the warm stone right next to her.

"It’s an original print of the old Bombay Fort maps!" she beamed, turning the fragile pages carefully.

"Do you know how many weeks we spent looking for these exact drawings in the college library?"

"Two months," he answered instantly, smiling.

"And you complained about the librarian every single day."

She burst out laughing, a bright and happy sound that seemed to chase away all the dust on the street. She turned her glowing face toward him, her eyes shining with the pure joy of finding something she truly loved. Rohan watched the morning sun light up her face. In that quiet second, he wanted nothing more than to reach out, brush the dust from her forehead, and finally tell her how much he loved her. But the fear of ruining their bond held him back, and he simply reached into his pocket to pay the book seller.

The Irani Cafe

They walked under the shady arches to their favorite corner table inside an old Irani cafe. High above them, slow wooden fans turned around and around, stirring the sweet smell of freshly baked bread, melted butter, and spiced tea. The small round marble table had chipped edges and brown stains from years of warm cups. The waiter brought them two glasses of hot Irani chai and a plate of warm bun maska without even being asked. Tara pulled a soft piece of bread apart with her fingers, dipped it into the sweet, cardamomy tea, and sighed with content.

"You know, Ro, people think this city is held together by concrete and money," she said, smiling as she chewed.

"But it’s really held together by small habits like this. If we didn’t come here every other Sunday, I think I would completely lose my mind."

"You make it sound like medicine," he teased gently, stirring his glass with a small spoon.

"It is medicine," she said honestly, looking right across the table at him.

"The rest of the world always wants something from me. At college, at home, everywhere. But with you, I don’t have to prove anything. I can just sit here, look tired, and be myself."

Her words felt both sweet and heartbreaking. He loved being the person she trusted above everyone else in the world. But deep down, he also knew what it meant: being her safe person meant she would never look at him as someone she could fall in love with. He was the ground she stood on, always there, but never seen as anything more.

"You never talk about yourself, Ro," she added, resting her chin on her hand to look at him closely.

"You spend every weekend helping me find old books or carrying my heavy bags. What about you? What do you want from your life?"

"I just want things to stay peaceful," he answered softly, his voice barely louder than the fan above them.

"I just want the people I care about to be happy and safe."

Tara looked at him with warm, affectionate eyes, gently shaking her head.

"You’re too gentle for your own good, Rohan. Sometimes I worry you’ll let the whole world pass you by while you’re busy taking care of everyone else."

The Blessing

Tara put her glass down on the saucer with a small clinking sound. Her easy, happy smile slowly faded away, replaced by an unusual stillness. She folded her paper napkin into a tight little square on the table, keeping her eyes glued to her hands.

"Ro... there’s something I need to ask you. Actually, I need your advice."

"You never need permission to ask me anything, Tara,"

Rohan said with a gentle smile, though his heart tightened in his chest.

"Kabir called me last night," she whispered, her fingers nervously smoothing the folded paper.

"He invited me to go to his family’s beach bungalow in Alibaug next weekend. Just the two of us. He said he wants to see if we can be something more than just friends."

In that single second, the entire cafe went dead silent. The noise of clinking spoons, the hum of the fan, the street sounds outside—everything vanished. Rohan felt a cold, crushing pain drop into his stomach like a stone.

"The hardest part of loving someone in secret isn’t watching them leave. It’s when they look you in the eyes and ask for your blessing to fall in love with someone else."

He looked at her shy, hopeful face. She wasn’t trying to hurt him. She trusted him so completely that she wanted his approval before she took a step into her own future. If he showed even a sliver of pain, or asked her not to go, their precious four years of friendship would break into pieces.

"Do you think I should go, Ro?" she asked, finally looking straight into his eyes.

"You’ve worked eighty hours a week without taking a single holiday, Tara,"

Rohan said, keeping his voice calm, steady, and full of warmth. He hid the breaking pieces of his heart behind a peaceful smile.

"Kabir is a good guy. He cares about you. You deserve to be happy. You should definitely go."

A bright, radiant smile broke across her face. Full of relief, she reached across the small table and grabbed both of his hands, holding them tightly with her warm fingers.

"Thank you, Ro! I was so nervous. You always know what to do. You really are the best friend in the world."

Rohan felt the warmth of her hands on his skin. He gently squeezed her fingers and smiled back at her, keeping his silent love buried deep where it had always lived, knowing that keeping her happy was the only way he could ever keep her near.

Chapter Three

Showing up, even when nobody asks.

The Two A.M. Draft

It was two o'clock in the morning, and the studio flat in Matunga was completely silent except for the steady whirr of an old ceiling fan. Outside the open balcony, the city had finally fallen asleep, leaving behind only the distant rumble of night trains carrying freight toward the docks. Across Rohan's drafting table, large sheets of tracing paper were held down with rusted metal paperweights.

Tara was sitting cross-legged on the faded cotton rug by his feet, her knees drawn tightly into an oversized knitted sweater. Her fingers were stained with black drawing ink, and an unfinished floor plan was spread out before her. In forty-eight hours, their final joint college portfolio had to be submitted, and she was on the verge of breaking down.

"The central roof beam is collapsing in the simulation, Ro," she whispered, her voice rough with exhaustion. "Look at the numbers. The load above the library courtyard drops by three whole inches under wind pressure. The entire model is ruined."

"It's not ruined, Tara," Rohan said gently from his wooden stool.

"I've reworked the math four times since midnight," she muttered, dropping her drafting pencil onto the rug. "Nothing balances. Maybe I'm just not good enough to make this work."

Rohan stepped down from his stool and knelt on the rug beside her. He picked up the pencil she had discarded, placed it softly between his palms, and reached for the scrap sheets covered in her anxious scribbles.

Matunga, 2:15 AM — a late-night fix to the roof calculations.

Without making a fuss, he had already redrawn the load distribution across three hidden steel columns while she had been resting her eyes earlier. He placed the freshly inked diagram over her sheet, aligning the corners with calm, steady fingers.

The Foundation You Don't See

Tara leaned forward, staring at the clean black lines he had drawn. She followed his calculations from the courtyard to the rear walls. He had found a way to support the roof without changing the design she had spent months working on.

"You fixed it," she breathed, looking up at him in pure wonder. "How long did this take you? Ro, you didn't sleep at all tonight, did you?"

"Your idea was right from the start, Tara," he answered quietly. "You just needed a hidden frame beneath it to take the pressure. The design belongs to you."

"Nothing belongs to me alone," she said, shaking her head. Her voice trembled slightly. "What would I ever do without you? Seriously. If you weren't standing behind me, my whole life would just collapse into unfinished drafts."

"You would still build it," he said with a faint smile. "You're stronger than you think."

Tara didn't answer. She slowly lowered her head, resting her forehead directly against the edge of his drafting table. Her breathing was uneven, heavy with all the weeks of unacknowledged stress, deadlines, and the quiet fear of failing in a city that gave second chances to no one.

Rohan sat beside her in the warm circle of yellow lamp light. He wanted so badly to reach out and pull her into his arms to press her head against his chest and tell her that as long as he was alive, she would never have to carry her burdens by herself. But he kept his hands still on the wood.

"Did Kabir call you tonight?" Rohan asked after a long while, hating himself the instant the question left his lips.

"He called at ten," she murmured into the wood, her voice muffled. "He told me to stop stressing over college work and just go to sleep. He said nobody cares about architectural perfection in the real world anyway."

"He was just trying to ease your mind," Rohan lied gently, defending the man he envied.

"No," she said, turning her cheek to face Rohan. Her dark eyes were bright with unshed tears. "He doesn't get it. He thinks this is just homework. He doesn't understand that this is who I am. Only you understand that, Ro. Only you."

Every word she spoke was like a dagger wrapped in silk. She recognized his soul completely, yet she had no idea that the heart inside it belonged entirely to her.

The Shawl

The room grew colder as the night stretched toward dawn. A sudden draft slipped through the balcony louvers, making the tracing papers rustle softly. Tara had stopped speaking. Her eyelids fluttered, grew heavy, and finally closed, her cheek resting peacefully against the drawing board beside his ink bottles.

Rohan stood up slowly, being careful not to let the floorboards creak. He walked to the wooden cupboard in the corner, pulled out his grandmother's thick brown wool shawl, and walked back to the desk. He shook it out softly in the dim light.

"The deepest love is rarely loud. It is found in the quiet moments when the other person is fast asleep, completely unaware of how carefully you are protecting their peace."

He draped the warm shawl over her shoulders, tucking the edges around her neck with infinite tenderness. His fingers lingered for just half a second near her ear, feeling the soft warmth of her skin. She shifted in her sleep, burying her chin deeper into the wool, letting out a small, satisfied breath.

"Ro... don't let the inks dry," she mumbled half-consciously in her dream.

"I'm taking care of it, Tara," he whispered into the stillness. "Go to sleep."

He sat down on the floor right beside her, leaning his back against the base of the table. He didn't pick up a book, and he didn't try to sleep. He simply stayed awake in the silence, guarding her rest while the blue light of 4:00 AM began to seep through the curtains, knowing that sunrise would bring the world back, and with it, all the distance they had to keep.

He watched the slow, steady rise and fall of her shoulders under the brown shawl. In these quiet, stolen hours of the night, she belonged to no one else-not to deadlines, not to the city, and not to Kabir. She was just Tara, sleeping beside him in the small room where his whole universe began and ended.

Chapter Four

A sudden downpour and nowhere else to go.

The Rain & The Blue Tarp

Two weeks later, the monsoon arrived over Mumbai with violent, sudden fury. At three in the afternoon, the sky above Tulsi Pipe Road turned from pale grey into the colour of wet slate. A freezing, salt-heavy gust of sea wind ripped through the railway bridges, and within five minutes, the heavens opened up in blinding sheets of rain.

Rohan and Tara were caught fifty yards from the station entrance, clutching their waterproof portfolio folders tightly against their chests. The road was already flooding with ankle-deep brown water, cars splashing waves against the crumbling sidewalks. Running for their lives, they dashed under the ragged blue plastic tarp of a small roadside tea stall.

"Bhaiya, two cutting chais, extra ginger!" Tara called out, laughing through breathless gasps.

"You're soaked to the bone, Tara," Rohan said, brushing heavy raindrops off his own forehead.

"So are you! Look at your shirt!" she pointed, her eyes bright and alive. "At least my drawings are dry. If these portfolio prints got ruined after four sleepless nights, I would have thrown myself into Mahim creek."

Water collected in the middle of the tarp and spilled over its edges whenever the wind shook it. Rain hammered against the plastic. The old tea-seller placed two small glass tumblers onto the wooden plank counter, the tea dark, piping hot, and smelling of crushed cardamom.

Tulsi Pipe Road, 3:40 PM — waiting out the rain under a blue tarp.

Tara picked up her glass with both hands, bringing it close to her chest to warm her shivering fingers. Her kurta was soaked at the sleeves, and she kept blinking rain out of her eyes.

The Crowded Shelter

The stall was tiny, and as the downpour turned even heavier, more strangers crowded under the makeshift canopy to escape the deluge. Rohan was pushed toward the very edge of the tarp, where heavy drops were falling steadily onto his right arm and soaking through his shoulder.

Tara noticed immediately. Without saying a word, she reached out, caught the wet cuff of his sleeve, and pulled him firmly toward her into the dry, sheltered center.

"Are you crazy, Ro?" she chided, her hand still holding tight to his wrist. "You're standing right under the gutter stream! Come here. Don't be an idiot."

"There isn't enough room, Tara," he said softly, trying not to invade her space.

"We fit," she replied firmly, pulling him until his arm was pressed against hers. "Stop trying to be brave all the time."

The space beneath the blue plastic was barely three feet wide. Packed together, Rohan could feel the cold dampness of her wet sleeve, but beneath it, the unmistakable radiant warmth of her body. The air was rich with the scent of wet mud, steaming milk tea, and the faint, sweet smell of her jasmine hair oil.

Tara took a sip of the scalding tea, letting out a small sigh of relief as the hot ginger warmed her throat. She looked out at the sheets of grey rain crashing onto the asphalt road, her face softened by the cozy shelter.

"Kabir came to pick me up today in his car, you know," she said quietly, watching the puddles ripple in the street.

Rohan felt his throat tighten. "Then why did you get soaked running across the road with me? You could have been in an air-conditioned car."

"Because I told him I'd already promised to take the train with you," she answered simply, turning her eyes up to look at him. "Besides, Kabir hates the rain. He rolls up his windows, puts on international podcasts, and complains about Mumbai traffic. He doesn't understand that you can't truly know this city if you don't let it drown you once in a while."

Rohan stared at her profile, his heart hammering against his ribs. It was terrifying how easily she gave him hope, without ever realizing what she was doing to him.

A Question Neither Asked

A sudden rush of wind shook the blue tarp, sending a shower of cold droplets splashing along the edges. Instinctively, Rohan raised his arm, placing his broad palm over her head to shield her face from the spray. His shoulder brushed against hers, his body leaning over her like a roof.

For several long seconds, neither of them moved. Tara didn't step away. She looked up at him through her damp eyelashes, and for the first time in four years, the easy laughter completely vanished from her expression. Her dark eyes were wide, deep, and searching, looking at him as if she were trying to read a language she had never noticed before.

"Why are you always like this, Ro?" she whispered, her voice barely carrying over the roar of the downpour.

"Like what?" his voice was quiet, unsteady.

"Always shielding me. Always giving me your coat, taking the heavy bags, standing in the rain so I stay dry... Why don't you ever ask for anything back?"

The moment hung in the damp air like thin glass, fragile and trembling on the edge of shattering. All the unsaid words of four long years rose into his throat like a tidal wave-every sleepless night, every train ride, every prayer he had ever whispered under his breath.

"There are moments when the truth is so close that a single honest breath could change everything. But fear whispers that speaking the truth might destroy the only piece of them you were allowed to hold."

Rohan looked into her wide, questioning eyes. If he told her now-if he told her that he did it because he loved her more than his own life-she might pull her hand away. The easy comfort, the laughter, the shared earphones on the 6:42 local would be gone forever. He couldn't bear the thought of a tomorrow where she was afraid to stand next to him.

"Because that's what friends do, Tara," Rohan said softly, giving her a gentle, reassuring smile that hid the tearing in his chest. "Someone has to keep you from catching a cold before tomorrow's jury."

The strange tension broke. Tara let out a small breath, though something quiet and lingering remained in the corners of her eyes. She smiled back, touching his wet jacket lightly with her hand. "The rain is slowing down. Come on, architect. The platform will be packed, and I'm not letting you miss the train."

They stepped out from beneath the blue tarp into the cool, dripping street. They walked side by side toward Dadar Station, their shoes splashing through the puddles, leaving two parallel sets of footprints behind them in the glistening monsoon mud.

Chapter Five

A weekend without Tara.

The Alibaug Weekend

Saturday felt strange without Tara around. Rohan made tea for two before he remembered she was away. He woke up at his usual hour, made tea for two out of pure muscle memory, and stared at the second ceramic mug sitting empty on the counter. Outside, Mumbai carried on as usual. He kept checking his phone, even though he knew she was busy.

He spent seven hours at his drafting table in Matunga, attempting to ink the foundation plans for an urban park. Every few minutes, his eyes drifted toward his phone. There were no frantic voice notes about missing rulers, no blurry photographs of street cats, no complaints about overcrowded buses. There was only a single text that had arrived at nine that morning: Reached safely. The sea breeze is beautiful, Ro. Talk to you soon!

"Have a good time, Tara," he had replied, staring at the screen for ten minutes before pressing send. "Don't think about work."

He had deleted four other drafts before sending that one. He had deleted: I already miss you.

By Sunday evening, the heat in Mumbai broke into a dull, grey drizzle. Rohan walked down to Dadar Station around six, not because he had anywhere to go, but because sitting inside his room felt like suffocating under a thick wool blanket. He stood on Platform 3, right beside the rusted iron pillar, watching the empty 6:42 train pull in without her.

Mandwa crossing — Tara away for the weekend, Rohan back in their usual places.

Surrounded by commuters, he noticed how much of his day depended on seeing her. He had never really admitted that to himself before. Without her laughter filling the space beside him, the city was just cold cement and metal tracks.

The Return

Monday morning brought Tara back to the college campus, but something in her step was quieter than usual. She arrived at the studio ten minutes before the morning lecture, wearing a soft pastel kurta and carrying a small handmade shell bracelet on her wrist. Kabir's silver car had dropped her at the main iron gates-Rohan had seen it from the second-floor library balcony.

When she walked into the drafting hall, her eyes immediately searched the rows of wooden tables until they locked onto him. She walked over, set her bag down on the stool beside his desk, and offered a soft, slightly tired smile.

"You're already inking the elevation drawings, Ro?" she asked, leaning against his desk.

"The jury is in three days," Rohan said gently, setting down his ruler. "How was Alibaug? Did you get to rest?"

"The bungalow was huge," she murmured, looking down at her fingernails. "Right on the beach. You could hear the waves from the living room all night. Kabir's friends were there too. They talked about foreign master's degrees, European design firms, and investments."

"Sounds like a different world," Rohan said, keeping his voice light and neutral.

"It was," she answered, but her tone lacked the excitement he had expected. "It felt like a catalog. Everything was neat, expensive, and polished. But I felt strange, Ro. Like I was wearing someone else's coat the whole weekend."

Rohan looked at her closely. Her skin was slightly sun-kissed from the beach air, but her eyes looked restless, carrying a faint shadow of unease that hadn't been there on Friday afternoon. He wanted to ask what Kabir had said to her, what promises had been made under the coastal stars, but the question felt dangerous and out of bounds.

"Did something happen, Tara?" he asked softly, leaning closer over his drawings.

"Kabir asked me to be his girlfriend," she said in a quiet whisper, looking directly into his eyes.

Rohan felt his lungs seize, as if the air in the studio had suddenly vanished. "What did you say?"

"I told him I needed a few days to think," she said, her fingers nervously twisting the shell bracelet. "He was surprised. He thought it was an obvious yes."

She paused, staring at the floorboards between their desks, looking more vulnerable than he had ever seen her before.

The Missing Piece

The drafting hall began to fill with students, the noise of dragging chairs and unrolling tracing paper echoing against the tall ceilings. The private bubble between them cracked, but Tara didn't move away from his table. She leaned in closer, her voice barely louder than a breath.

"Can we go to your terrace tonight, Ro?" she asked, her voice tight with an unspoke need. "Just for an hour. I can't think inside my house right now. Everything feels too crowded."

"Of course," Rohan said without a second of hesitation. "Whenever you need."

"Thank you," she whispered, letting out a small, shaky breath. "You're the only person who doesn't pressure me to have an answer."

All afternoon during classes, Rohan worked like a machine, but his mind was somewhere far away, trapped in a storm of confusion and quiet agony. Why hadn't she said yes to Kabir immediately? Kabir had everything a young woman could dream of-wealth, charm, a grand future laid out in straight, effortless lines. What was holding her back?

"Hope is a dangerous visitor. It slips through the smallest crack in the door, making you believe that a miracle might happen, even when reason tells you that you are only the waiting room of someone else's life."

He scolded himself for even allowing that tiny flicker of hope to breathe inside his ribs. It was foolish to imagine that her hesitation had anything to do with him. She was just overwhelmed by the sudden change in her world. She was his best friend, seeking shelter from the wind, and his only job was to be the shelter she needed-nothing more, nothing less.

When the evening bell rang at five, they took the local train back together in near silence. For the first time, they didn't share the earphones. Tara rested her head against the door frame, watching the sodium lamps flicker on along the highway, while Rohan stood beside her, his hand gripping the metal strap as tight as his beating heart.

The sky was turning a deep, bruised purple as they reached Matunga. The air was heavy with the promise of more rain, and neither of them spoke as they climbed the narrow concrete stairs leading up to the roof of his building.

Chapter Six

A conversation on the terrace.

The Silent Terrace

The terrace of the four-story Matunga building was where they had spent countless evenings over the last four years. The concrete floor was rough and uneven, holding small puddles of rainwater from the morning shower. Across the low parapet wall, the sprawling rooftops of central Mumbai stretched out toward the distant, glowing skyline of Lower Parel.

Rohan had brought up two mugs of hot instant coffee and an old woven straw mat. Tara kicked off her sandals and sat down near the edge, pulling her knees up to her chest. The cool evening breeze ruffled her hair, carrying the earthy smell of damp cement and blooming frangipani from the street below.

"Here," Rohan said, handing her a steaming mug. "Two spoons of sugar, just the way you like it."

"You always remember," she said softly, wrapping both hands around the ceramic mug to feel the warmth. "At the bungalow, Kabir kept ordering black espresso from the machine. He said real architects drink bitter coffee to stay sharp. It tasted terrible, Ro."

"You could have just asked for milk and sugar, Tara," Rohan laughed gently, sitting down beside her on the mat.

"I didn't want to look childish in front of his friends," she sighed, looking down into the dark coffee. "Everyone there was so certain of themselves. They talked about who they were going to be five years from now, and I just sat there holding a bitter cup, wondering why I couldn't just be happy looking at the sea."

Matunga terrace, 7:15 PM — two cups of coffee and the city below.

Lights came on across the buildings as evening settled over Mumbai. They sat in comfortable silence for several minutes, the wind blowing the steam from their cups toward the open sky.

The Confession of Doubt

Tara set her mug down on the low ledge. She turned to face Rohan, drawing one leg under herself on the mat. The last of the daylight caught her face as she turned toward him.

"On Saturday night, we walked down to the shore after dinner," she began, her voice barely louder than the rustle of the wind. "The water was dark and roaring. Kabir held my hand. He's very sweet, Ro. He told me he's liked me since our third-year exhibition. He told me he wants us to work together, travel together, build a life together."

Rohan looked out at the Lower Parel towers, forcing himself not to flinch. "That sounds like everything anyone could want, Tara. Kabir has a brilliant future."

"I know," she said, but her voice cracked slightly. "Then why did my chest feel so heavy while he was saying it?"

Rohan turned his head slowly to look at her. "What do you mean?"

"While he was holding my hand and talking about our five-year plan, all I could think about was you, Ro."

The world seemed to stop spinning on its axis. The roar of the distant trains, the wind in the frangipani branches, the sound of his own breathing-everything dropped into dead, frozen silence. Rohan stared at her, terrified that his ears were playing cruel tricks on his desperate mind.

"You thought about me?" he asked, his voice shaking despite all his efforts to steady it.

"I thought about how you would have laughed at the pretentious art in the hall," she said, her eyes glistening in the dim twilight. "I thought about how, whenever I'm scared or overwhelmed, you never give me a speech about being strong. You just quietly take the heavy bag off my shoulder. I thought about how Kabir doesn't know what I look like when I'm completely broken... and how you've seen every single tear I've cried for four years."

Tara reached out and placed her small, warm palm over his knuckles on the woven mat. Her touch was trembling, sending a jolt of fire and ice through every nerve in his body.

"I'm terrified, Ro," she whispered, a tear finally escaping her eye and rolling down her cheek. "I'm terrified that if I say yes to Kabir, I'll lose the only person who actually knows who I am."

What She Was Afraid to Say

Her hand was resting on his. Her tears were reflecting the city light. Never in all their years had they stood so dangerously close to the edge of the truth. A single sentence-just three words-could have shattered the four-year wall between them. He could have turned his hand over, laced his fingers through hers, and pulled her into his arms where she had belonged since the very first day they met.

"Sometimes the greatest bravery is not confessing your love. It is holding your peace when you realize the other person is asking for shelter, not a fire that might burn the only home they have left."

Rohan looked at her wet eyes, at her trembling lip, at the fear on her face. She wasn't confessing romantic love to him-she was having a panic attack about growing up, about leaving the safe harbor of their college years, about stepping into the real world with a man who expected her to be an adult. If he confessed his love now, he wouldn't be saving her; he would be trapping her inside his own selfish desires.

"You won't lose me, Tara," Rohan said softly, his voice full of an immense, heartbreaking tenderness. He turned his hand over and gently wrapped his fingers around hers, squeezing with steady reassurance. "Never. Not for Kabir, not for a job, not for anyone."

"Do you promise?" she asked, her voice small and childlike.

"I promised you on the train over Mahim creek, remember?" he smiled, brushing a tear from her cheek with the back of his thumb. "I don't break promises. If Kabir makes you happy, say yes. If he doesn't, walk away. But don't make your decision out of fear of losing me. I will always be standing right where you left me."

Tara let out a long, shuddering breath and leaned her forehead against his shoulder. Her hair brushed his neck, soft and smelling of jasmine and salt air. Rohan held his breath, closing his eyes against the sting behind his lids, letting her rest against him while the night breeze washed over the rooftop.

Down below on the street, cars honked and people rushed home through the gathering dark. Rohan wrapped his arm gently around her shoulders, holding her through the storm, loving her enough to let her go, and praying that the night would never end.

Chapter Seven

Their final project, and the people watching.

The Annual Exhibition

The grand convocation hall of the architecture college had been stripped bare of its wooden desks. Long rows of white display partitions now crisscrossed the room, illuminated by sharp spotlight fixtures hung from the teak beams overhead. It was the night of the final thesis showcase, the one evening where months of sleepless nights and bleeding fingers were put on trial before the city's leading architects.

Tara stood beside their joint display board, wearing a deep indigo handloom kurta with silver ear studs. She had spent the last two hours polishing the balsa-wood scale model of the Matunga community center, her hands fluttering nervously over the miniature glass courtyard that Rohan had reinforced for her at two in the morning.

"The jury chairman just walked into the eastern gallery, Ro," she murmured, her voice tight with panic. "Look at him. He's already crossed his arms. He looks like he's about to tear someone apart."

"He always crosses his arms, Tara," Rohan whispered, standing half a step behind her with a cup of cold water. "It has nothing to do with design. His back hurts from judging four hundred students every July. Drink this."

"I can't swallow," she said, though she took the cup anyway, her fingers brushing his with cold tremor.

Rohan straightened the corner of her board against the easel. He stayed beside her while the professors moved through the room, answering questions when she needed him and letting her speak for herself.

Hall of Arches, 6:30 PM — the final thesis jury.

Then, the crowd near the entry arches parted slightly, and a tall figure in a tailored linen blazer walked into the hall. It was Kabir, holding a slender bouquet of white lilies wrapped in brown kraft paper, looking effortlessly out of place among the ink-stained students.

The Lilies on the Pedestal

Kabir walked directly toward their display stall, his footsteps confident on the polished stone floor. When Tara saw him, a faint blush rose up her neck, though her fingers tightened nervously around the edge of her drawing folder. Kabir smiled warmly, stepped past Rohan without acknowledging him, and handed the lilies to Tara.

"For the most brilliant designer in this room," Kabir said smoothly, kissing her lightly on the cheek. "I parked outside on the promenade. When this jury nonsense wraps up, I've booked a table for us at the Yacht Club to celebrate."

"Kabir, the jury hasn't even arrived at our stall yet," Tara murmured, holding the heavy flowers awkwardly against her side. "We still have forty minutes of defense to get through."

"You'll breeze through it," he laughed, dismissing her fear with an easy wave of his hand. "It's an undergraduate showcase, Tara. In two months, nobody will remember the grades anyway. What matters is what comes after."

Rohan stood two paces back, his hands folded behind his back, his face a completely blank wall.

Tara looked over Kabir's shoulder at Rohan. Her eyes held an unspoken apology, an awkward plea for him not to disappear into the crowd. But Rohan didn't look away. He held her gaze steadily, nodding with that quiet, reassuring grace that told her she didn't need to explain anything.

When the jury panel finally reached stall number fourteen, Kabir stepped back against the gallery wall, checking his watch every five minutes. But Rohan stepped forward to the side of the table, pointer in hand, ready to flip the heavy cross-sectional drawings the second Tara paused for breath.

"Who designed the cantilevers above the public hall?" the stern senior examiner demanded, leaning over the balsa model with his spectacles lowered.

"The form and public flow are mine, sir," Tara began, her voice gaining strength with every word. "And the concealed structural spine was resolved by Rohan. Without his load calculations, the roof wouldn't hold its own weight."

The examiner paused, looked up at Rohan, and then nodded slowly with genuine respect. For fifteen minutes, Tara spoke like a woman inspired, and every time she needed a drawing, a reference number, or a nod of courage, Rohan was right there before she even had to ask.

Across the Display Board

When the jury finally concluded with high praise and firm handshakes, the tension in the exhibition hall broke into loud laughter, corks popping from cheap sparkling wine, and tired students hugging each other in relief. Kabir stepped forward immediately, wrapping his arm around Tara's waist and pulling her toward the exit.

"See? I told you it was nothing to lose sleep over," Kabir said proudly, loud enough for nearby professors to hear. "Come on, Tara. The car is waiting, and the table won't hold past eight."

"Wait, Kabir," Tara said, twisting gently out of his grip. She turned back toward the stall. "Rohan hasn't packed down the drafting sheets yet. I need to help him pack the portfolio box."

"He can manage the sheets, Tara," Kabir replied with an easy, careless smile. "He lives right next to campus anyway. Let's go celebrate your victory."

Rohan was already rolling the first heavy sheet of tracing paper into its black protective cylinder, his fingers moving methodically, snapping the plastic caps into place without looking up.

"The hardest goodbye is not the one spoken at the station platform. It is the one where someone walks away beside another, leaving you with the heavy tools of the dream you built together."

"Go, Tara," Rohan said, looking up with a calm, gentle smile that masked the cold tearing inside his ribs. "You earned this. Kabir has been waiting for an hour. I'll pack the model into the storage locker. It'll take me ten minutes."

Tara hesitated. She held the white lilies loosely in her left hand, her eyes darting between Kabir's impatient smile and Rohan's solitary figure standing by the wooden table. For a second, her lips parted as if she were about to say no, as if she were about to drop the flowers and stay behind on the dusty floor.

"Are you sure, Ro?" she asked, her voice quiet and uncertain.

"I'm sure," he replied. "I'll see you tomorrow morning for the final roll call."

She gave him one last lingering look before letting Kabir lead her through the tall teak doors into the warm Mumbai night. Rohan stood alone in the emptying gallery, the harsh white spotlight beaming down on his back, carefully wrapping the balsa-wood courtyard in soft tissue paper so it wouldn't break.

Chapter Eight

A call after midnight.

The Coffee Cup Confession

It was nearly midnight when Rohan's phone buzzed on the drafting table in his room. He had already showered, put away the thesis folders, and sat down with a cup of black tea to read through an old monograph on stone masonry. The caller ID flashed with Tara's name, and his heart leaped into his throat before he could stop it.

"Are you awake?" her voice came through the receiver, quiet and strangely flat.

"I'm awake," he answered instantly. "Where are you? Aren't you at the Yacht Club?"

"I left an hour ago," she said. "I'm sitting at the Sea Green cafe on Marine Drive. Can you come down? Just for half an hour. I don't want to go home yet."

He didn't ask why. He threw on his worn cotton jacket, grabbed his keys from the wooden bowl by the door, and walked briskly down the flight of stairs into the humid night. The streets were mostly empty. Streetlights shone on the wet road as Rohan hurried toward Marine Drive.

Marine Drive, 12:15 AM — a late coffee and a conversation that could not wait.

When he reached the small glass-fronted cafe opposite the promenade, he saw her sitting alone by the corner window. The bouquet of lilies was nowhere in sight. Before her sat an untouched glass mug of cold black coffee, its surface undisturbed under the dull yellow neon sign of the cafe.

The Untouched Table

Rohan pulled back the aluminum-legged chair across from her and sat down. The table had water rings on it, and the chair scraped loudly as he pulled it out. Tara looked up, her face pale under the fluorescent tubes, her dark hair slightly damp from the ocean spray drifting across the sea wall.

"You didn't drink your coffee," Rohan said softly, nodding toward the dark mug.

"I ordered it because I wanted an excuse to sit by the window," she whispered, her voice rough with fatigue. "The dinner was terrible, Ro. Kabir brought three of his partners from the real estate fund. They spent the entire night talking about land acquisition in Goa and how much money they're going to flip off coastal resorts."

"Did you tell him how you felt?"

"I tried," she said, shaking her head slowly. "I told him our thesis was about preserving community memory, about keeping old public spaces alive. He laughed, squeezed my shoulder in front of everyone, and said, 'Tara's still an idealist, she'll grow out of it once we start bidding on actual tenders.'"

Her voice cracked on the final sentence. She looked out through the glass toward the crashing waves of the Arabian Sea, where the tide was smashing violently against the giant tetrapods lining the coast.

Rohan felt a surge of cold anger tighten in his chest, not for himself, but for the casual cruelty with which a man like Kabir could diminish everything she loved. Yet he knew that turning that anger into words would only make him look bitter and petty.

"You don't have to grow out of it, Tara," Rohan said, leaning closer so his words were firm and clear. "Your idealism is the only reason your drawings have life. Don't let anyone make you feel small for caring about things that can't be bought on an invoice."

She turned her face toward him, her eyelashes dark and wet. "Why are you always the one who says the words I need to hear, Ro? Why is it never the person I'm supposed to be falling in love with?"

The question hung between them like an open wound, raw and bleeding across the narrow cafe table.

The Walk Outside

Rohan stared at the small silver ring on her thumb, unable to speak. The air in the cafe felt suddenly thick, suffocating beneath the weight of everything they had left unsaid over four years of train rides and quiet afternoons. He could have told her then-that Kabir was not the person she was looking for because the person she needed had been sitting across from her all along.

"There is no loneliness quite like being loved for your silence, when every heartbeat inside you is screaming to be heard."

Tara reached across the table, her cold fingers wrapping tightly around his wrist. Her skin was freezing, chilled by the air conditioning and the midnight sea spray, but her grip was desperate, holding onto him as if he were the only solid post left standing in a collapsing world.

"Walk with me along the sea wall," she whispered, standing up from the chair before he could answer. "Just for fifteen minutes. The wind will clear my head."

"Tara, it's past twelve," he murmured, though he was already rising to pay the waiter at the counter.

"Just fifteen minutes, Rohan. Please. Don't leave me alone tonight."

They crossed the broad, empty road of Marine Drive, stepping up onto the wide concrete promenade. The ocean breeze slammed into them with tremendous force, smelling of salt water, diesel exhaust, and wet stone. The sea crashed against the dark rocks below, throwing cold mist into the air that settled like tiny diamonds in Tara's hair.

They walked side by side in silence, their shoulders brushing with every fifth step. Tara tucked her hands deep into the pockets of her kurta, staring out into the pitch-black horizon where distant container ships sat anchored like ghost lights.

Rohan kept his pace matched to hers, walking on the street side so his body blocked the strongest gusts of the sea wind. He knew that tomorrow morning the world would demand answers from both of them-interviews, firms, decisions that would scatter their lives across different continents. But for tonight, with the salt water on their lips and the midnight road empty behind them, he was still her anchor, and that was enough to keep his heart beating in the dark.

Chapter Nine

Offers, decisions, and a possible move away.

The Job Offers

By August, placement letters and interview schedules had taken over the college notice board. The campus notice board was crowded with formal interview schedules and placement letters stamped by corporate firms. For four years, college had given them a routine. Now the letters on the notice board were asking them to decide what came next.

Rohan stood under the stone colonnade outside the dean's office, holding a cream-colored envelope. It was an offer letter from a respected heritage conservation studio based right in Fort, South Bombay. The salary was modest, but it meant restoring stone libraries, ancient arches, and old port buildings-the exact work he had loved since his first semester.

"Did you get it, Ro?" Tara's voice came from the steps behind him.

He turned around, folding the paper into his pocket with a gentle smile. "The heritage firm accepted my drawings. Starting next month."

"Oh, Rohan!" she cried out, her face lighting up with genuine joy. She reached forward and hugged him tightly, her arms warm around his shoulders. "That's your dream! Nobody understands old stone the way you do. I'm so proud of you."

Rohan held her for just a brief, precious second before she stepped back. That was when he noticed the thick, expensive blue folder tucked tightly under her own arm, embossed with the gold seal of a multinational firm.

The college quadrangle — the week their first job offers arrived.

"What about you, Tara?" he asked quietly, his eyes falling to the blue folder in her hands. The happy expression on her face flickered, replaced by a sudden, anxious hesitation that made his stomach sink.

The Bangalore Contract

Tara looked down at the folder, her fingers smoothing the gold foil letters. She didn't open it. She walked over to the stone ledge of the quadrangle and sat down, staring out at the rain-soaked lawn where juniors were carrying white styrofoam boards.

"It's from a global design consultancy," she murmured, her voice barely carrying over the distant rumble of thunder. "Kabir's father arranged the interview. They offered me a junior associate role in their sustainable housing division."

"That's incredible, Tara," Rohan said honestly, walking over to sit beside her. "Their projects are known worldwide. That's everything you wanted when we wrote our thesis statement."

"The position isn't in Mumbai, Ro," she said softly, turning her dark eyes toward him.

Rohan felt the smile freeze on his lips. "Where is it?"

"Bangalore. The main design lab is there. Kabir is moving too-his family is opening an investment branch on MG Road. He wants us to sign the contracts together next week."

The quadrangle was completely silent for several seconds. A sparrow hopped along the wet stone ledge, shaking droplets of rain from its wings. Rohan looked at the grey grass, trying to process the finality of the word. *Bangalore.* Six hundred miles away. A city of new highways, different climates, and completely different train tracks where he would never be standing by the pillar.

He looked at her hands. The shell bracelet from Alibaug was still around her wrist, but her fingers were trembling as she held the blue folder. She didn't look like someone who had won the golden prize; she looked like someone standing on the plank of a ship, waiting for the cold water below.

"Have you signed it?" he asked, keeping his tone carefully steady.

"Not yet," she whispered. "Kabir thinks I'm crazy for waiting. He says this is the kind of job people kill for right out of graduation. He says staying in Mumbai will just trap me in small, underpaid conservation jobs that lead nowhere."

"Kabir only looks at the ceiling, Tara. He doesn't understand why people care about the roots."

She gave a faint, sad smile, leaning her shoulder lightly against his arm. "I know. That's why I came to find you first."

The Decision

They sat on the cold stone ledge as the afternoon classes ended. Other students came spilling out into the hallways, their laughter loud, careless, and full of the easy confidence of young people who thought their friendships would last forever without effort. Tara drew her knees up to her chest, resting her chin on her sweater.

"Tell me what to do, Ro," she whispered, her eyes pleading with him. "If you tell me to stay, I'll reject the offer today. I'll apply to the Fort studio with you. We can share a drafting desk like we always do."

The words hit him like a physical blow. The temptation rose up inside him with terrifying power. All he had to do was say the word. One sentence-*Stay with me*-and she would remain in Mumbai. She would be on the 6:42 local, she would be at the Flora Fountain book stalls on Sundays, and she would be in his life every single day.

"The most painful test of love is not holding someone tight when they want to stay. It is refusing to clip their wings just so you won't have to face the empty sky alone."

He looked at her gifted hands, at the brilliant mind that had designed sustainable courtyards that made senior professors stop in awe. If she stayed for him, she would be staying out of comfort and fear. And ten years from now, when the small heritage projects grew monotonous, she would look at the sky and wonder what kind of architect she could have become.

"You have to take the job, Tara," Rohan said, each word feeling like a piece of glass sliding down his throat, yet his voice was clear, soft, and unwavering.

Tara stared at him, her eyes glistening. "Do you really want me to leave?"

"I want you to become the architect you were meant to be," he answered, reaching over to gently close the folder in her hands. "You've spent four years dreaming of building sustainable cities. This firm has the resources to let you do that. Don't stay in Mumbai just because it feels safe. You're too big for safety."

A single tear spilled over her dark eyelashes, tracking slowly down her cheek. She didn't say anything. She just reached over and took his hand, holding it so tightly that her fingernails pressed into his palm, her silence carrying all the grief of an ending they both knew was coming.

Chapter Ten

One last ride on the 6:42.

The Last Train of Season One

Friday was the last day they were officially architecture students. The keys to their drafting lockers had been handed in, the graduation certificates were rolled up in blue velvet tubes, and the college gates closed behind them for the last time. By evening, the monsoon clouds had parted into a deep, burning orange sunset that bathed Dadar Station in warm bronze light.

At 6:35 PM, Rohan walked onto Platform 3. He felt nervous in a way he couldn't explain, and kept checking the time even though he was early. He walked down the platform with slow, deliberate steps and stopped beside the rusted iron pillar, under the old ceiling fan that had watched over them for four hundred days.

"You didn't think I'd let Kabir drive me home today, did you?"

Rohan turned around. Tara was standing on the overbridge stairs, walking down toward him with her black drafting tube over her shoulder. She was wearing the same simple cotton kurta she had worn on their very first day of college, her silver earrings catching the sunset glow, holding two paper cups of cutting chai.

Dadar Station, 6:42 PM — their last train ride before Tara left for Bangalore.

"I knew you'd take the train," Rohan said softly, taking one cup from her hand. The tea was too hot to drink straight away. Tara blew across the top of hers, as she always did.

The Shared Earphone One Last Time

The 6:42 Fast Local pulled in with its familiar iron rumble. For the final time, Rohan stepped ahead of her, his body forming the same quiet shield against the surge of travelers. They moved inside together and found their spot by the open metal doorway, the warm evening air rushing in as the train accelerated out of Dadar.

Without a word, Tara reached into her pocket. She pulled out the white wired earphones, untangled them with trembling fingers, and handed the left bud to Rohan. He plugged it into his ear. It was the same old acoustic playlist they had started four years ago on their first week of studio work.

"My flight to Bangalore is on Sunday morning, Ro," she said, her voice competing with the clatter of the wheels on the rails.

"I know," he murmured, staring out at the slums and high-rises blurring past Mahim creek. "Have you packed your drawing tubes?"

"Most of them. I left the Matunga community center drawings in your room. I want you to keep the portfolio box."

Rohan turned his head to look at her. "That was your thesis, Tara. You earned top marks for it."

"It's ours," she said firmly, her eyes searching his. "It belongs in the room where it was made. With the person who made it stand."

The train crossed over the dark waters of Mahim creek. The sun had dipped below the horizon, leaving a purple bruise across the evening sky. Tara leaned her head against Rohan's shoulder-not by accident, and not because the train had swayed. She leaned into him fully, resting her temple against his cotton shirt as if she were anchoring herself to the earth for the last time.

Rohan closed his eyes, his breathing shallow. He could feel the soft texture of her hair, the steady warmth of her forehead against his chest, and the faint scent of jasmine oil. In four years, she had never leaned on him like this on a crowded train.

"Rohan?" she whispered, her voice barely audible over the guitar chords in their ears.

"Yeah?"

"Why didn't you ever ask me to stay?"

The question shattered through him like cold glass, freezing the blood in his veins.

After the Train

The train began to slow down as the yellow lights of Bandra Station slid alongside the windows. Commuters were already shifting their bags, lining up at the door. Her head was still resting against his shoulder, waiting for the answer he had guarded with his life.

"Sometimes love is not about giving yourself the ending you wanted. It is about loving someone so completely that their freedom matters more than your own happiness."

He looked down at the soft curve of her forehead. He could have told her everything right then-that he had loved her on the rainy days under the blue tarp, in the quiet hours of two in the morning over blueprints, and every single evening beneath the rusted fan. But if he said it now, as she stood on the brink of her dream in Bangalore, he would only burden her with guilt.

"Because your life is out there, Tara," Rohan said softly, his voice trembling with quiet grace. "And you deserve to fly without anyone holding the string."

Tara lifted her head slowly. Her eyes were completely flooded with tears. She didn't speak, but in that long, unbroken gaze, something shifted between them forever. She finally saw the truth that had lived in his silence for four years. She took off the white earphone from her ear, wrapped the wire carefully around her palm, and pressed the small white plastic piece into his hand, closing his fingers around it.

"Keep it, Ro," she whispered as the train doors slid open to the crowded platform. "So you remember to listen to our songs when I'm gone."

"I will," he nodded, his vision blurring with tears he refused to let fall.

She stepped out onto the platform. She walked five paces, stopped, and turned back to look at him through the open door frame. The train horn sounded a long, mournful blast, and the heavy metal wheels began to roll forward again, carrying him away into the Mumbai night.

Rohan stood by the open door, his fist clenched tightly around the white earphone in his pocket. The city rushed past in dark, blurry streaks, but in his heart, he was still standing beneath the rusted iron fan on Platform 3, waiting for her to come down the stairs.

Chapter Eleven

A new beginning, drawn one line at a time.

Part I — The First Studio

It was a blindingly bright Monday in August when Rohan first set foot in the grand old architecture hall. The college building was built of basalt and Burma teak, smelling of century-old dust, machine oil, and fresh wood shavings. High above, rows of massive fan blades spun slowly, doing nothing to cool the nervous sweat of sixty newly admitted freshmen who stood clutching brand-new wooden drawing boards.

Rohan had arrived forty minutes before anyone else. His father had bought him a sturdy wooden T-square and a second-hand set of drafting pens from a stationery shop in Dadar. He picked a quiet corner desk by the western window, where the afternoon sun would slant gently without causing glare on the vellum.

“Is this seat taken, or are you reserving it for your ego?” a girl asked.

Rohan startled, dropping his roll of masking tape. Standing right in front of the neighboring stool was a girl holding an enormous black drafting tube that was nearly half her height. She was wearing a loose mustard-yellow cotton kurta, with a dark blue ink smudge already marking her thumb.

“No,” Rohan mumbled, picking up his tape from the floorboards. “Nobody’s sitting there. It’s free.”

“Good,” she said, dumping her heavy tube onto the wooden table with a loud clatter that made several heads turn. “I’m Tara. And if you’re the kind of person who leaves graphite dust all over the shared board, tell me right now so I can move.”

Rohan stared at her, blinking. She wasn’t angry, but her dark eyes were dancing with an effortless, fearless energy that instantly caught him off guard. He simply shook his head, tucked his chin down, and whispered his name.

Part II — The Broken Acrylic

The first lecture was brutal. Professor Kulkarni, an elderly architect who had taught for thirty years, walked into the studio with a heavy wooden stick. He demanded that every student pin down an imperial sheet of cartridge paper and draft fifty parallel lines spaced exactly two millimeters apart—without using a ruler for measurement.

“If your line wavers, you tear the sheet!” the professor barked. “Architecture is precision! If your hands shake today, your walls will collapse tomorrow! Start!”

The room broke into panicked movement. Paper crinkled, drafting tape tore, and sixty pencils began to scratch desperately against the boards. Rohan worked with steady, patient calm. He had practiced drafting with his father for weeks; his lines were clean, light, and completely straight.

Beside him, however, things were not going well. Tara was struggling with her acrylic T-square. It was an expensive, clear plastic ruler, but as she tried to tighten the wingnut against the edge of the board, there was a sharp, brittle snap. The clear blade broke right off the wooden head, clattering to the floor in two jagged pieces.

“Oh, no...” Tara gasped under her breath. Her cheeks flushed bright crimson. Professor Kulkarni was only three desks away, his heavy boots thumping closer.

“What is that noise over there?” the professor yelled, turning his head.

Tara froze, her eyes wide with panic. Tearing a sheet on the first day was bad enough; being caught without working tools meant being thrown out of the studio before lunch. Rohan didn’t pause. Without saying a word, he slid his own wooden T-square across the two inches of space between their desks, pushing it right under her hand.

“Take it,” Rohan whispered quickly, keeping his head down. “Draw your margins now.”

“What about you?” she breathed, terrified.

“I’ve already finished the outer border,” Rohan whispered. “Just use it. Fast.”

Tara grabbed the wooden ruler and drew her first clean line just as the professor walked past their desk. The old man glanced at their table, nodded once at the straight pencil lines, and moved down the row without stopping. Tara let out a long, shuddering breath, her shoulders sagging in relief.

Part III — The Shared Desk

When the bell rang for the one o’clock lunch break, the studio emptied out into the loud canteen courtyard. But Rohan stayed in his chair, quietly sharpening his pencils over a small sheet of sandpaper. He had brought two simple vegetable sandwiches wrapped in silver foil from home.

Tara didn’t leave either. She picked up the two broken pieces of her acrylic ruler from the floor, staring at them with a frustrated frown. Then, she looked up at Rohan. The sharp, defensive wall she had put up that morning was completely gone, replaced by a soft, genuine warmth.

“You saved my life today, Ro,” she said quietly, setting the broken plastic aside.

“It was just a ruler,” Rohan replied, looking up with a faint smile. “He would have kicked you out. Kulkarni has a terrible temper on Mondays.”

“It wasn’t just a ruler,” she insisted, leaning her chin on her hands to look across at him. “Everyone else was too terrified to look up from their paper. You didn’t even know me, and you gave me your only tool. Why did you do that?”

Rohan looked down at his sandwiches. He had spent the morning watching her struggle, noticing how passionately she gripped her pencil, how her eyebrows knitted together when she was focused. He recognized someone who took drawing as seriously as he did.

“I just hate seeing a good sheet get wasted,” he murmured.

Tara burst out laughing. It was the first time he had heard her real laugh—loud, honest, and completely uninhibited. She reached into her tote bag, pulled out a small plastic box of dry potato bhajiyas, and set it right on top of his silver foil.

“Half of my lunch is yours,” she declared, pulling her stool closer until the wooden edges of their desks were touching. “And starting tomorrow, we’re sharing this T-square until I buy a new one. Deal?”

“Deal,” he said with a smile. And in that small, sunlit studio corner, an easy partnership quietly began.

Part IV — The Quiet Pact

By the end of their first month in college, they had become an inseparable fixture of the western studio corner. Other students formed loud groups, gossiping about senior parties or comparing expensive laptops. But Rohan and Tara lived in a world made entirely of 2B graphite, tracing paper, and cutting mats.

They balanced each other with effortless teamwork. Tara had wild, creative design instincts—she dreamed of spiraling ramps, open glass ceilings, and courtyards that welcomed the monsoon breeze. But she was chaotic, messy with her tools, and often forgot to calculate basic wall thicknesses. Rohan, on the other hand, was meticulous. He could calculate structural loads in his head, kept his pencils razor-sharp, and made her ambitious ideas stand on solid ground.

“If I build this ramp, Ro, will it hold?” she would ask, pointing her ink-stained finger at an ambitious sketch.

“Not with brick,” Rohan would say, leaning over her board. “You need steel I-beams here. If you add two hidden columns beneath the stair landing, the whole thing will float.”

“See? You’re my engineer,” she would grin, tapping his forehead with her pencil eraser. “I invent the madness, and you keep the roof from falling on my head.”

Real friendship does not start with grand declarations. It begins when two people quietly realize that working together is easier than facing the storm alone.

Every afternoon at four, when the studio air grew thick and heavy, they walked down the grand wooden staircase together. They didn’t go to the trendy cafes where the wealthy students gathered. They walked three hundred yards down the pavement to an old Irani tea stall near the railway crossing, where a glass of cutting chai cost only five rupees.

Standing by the hot stove, blowing steam from the glass, Tara would tell him about her childhood in Pune, her fears of failing the semester, and her dream of building housing that ordinary families could afford. Rohan would listen quietly, drinking his tea, grateful to have found someone who understood why buildings mattered so deeply to him.

Chapter Twelve

Some nights bring two people closer than words ever could.

Part I — The First All-Nighter

October brought their first major mid-term submission: a comprehensive design for a community health clinic. The deadline was set for nine o’clock on a Friday morning. By Thursday midnight, the main college studio was a disaster zone of discarded tracing paper, empty tea cups, and exhausted students sleeping with their heads on drafting boards.

Tara was on the verge of breaking down. At one in the morning, while trying to erase a smudge on her main floor plan, her electric eraser caught the corner of the thin gateway paper, tearing a three-inch jagged hole right through her hospital ward. She dropped her head onto the board, her shoulders shaking with exhausted frustration.

“I can’t redraw it, Ro,” she groaned, her face buried in her arms. “It took me eighteen hours to ink these walls. My hands are cramping. I’m just going to fail the jury and pack my bags for Pune.”

Rohan set down his own finished sheets. He had been done since midnight. Without a second of hesitation, he pulled his stool next to hers. He took a bottle of drafting adhesive, carefully aligned a patch of matching vellum beneath the tear, and smoothed the edges with a bone folder until the seam was almost invisible.

“Look at me, Tara,” he said firmly, tapping the side of her drawing board.

She lifted her head, her dark eyes red-rimmed and tired, looking at him with desperate exhaustion.

“You’re not failing anything,” he said calmly. “Go wash your face with cold water. Drink some water. I will re-ink the missing wall lines. When the jury walks in at nine, your clinic will be completely fine. Trust me.”

She stared at him for several seconds, her chest heaving with quiet breaths, before nodding slowly. And as Rohan bent over her board with his 0.2 pen, carefully matching every stroke of her handwriting, they both realized they had become a team that wouldn't let the other fall.

Part II — The Monsoon on the Overbridge

The jury on Friday morning went better than either of them expected. The external examiners praised Tara’s clinic for its generous courtyards, giving her an ‘A’ grade that made her breathe easily for the first time in three weeks. When they finally walked out of the college gates at four that afternoon, the semester had paused for a short break, and both of them were running entirely on adrenaline.

As they reached the entrance of Dadar Station, the October sky suddenly split open. It was a torrential monsoon downpour that turned the road into a rushing stream in ten minutes. Commuters ran for cover under bridge arches. Rohan and Tara were caught right in the middle of the long concrete footbridge that connected the Central and Western lines.

“My portfolio sheets!” Tara yelled, clutching her cardboard folder against her chest.

Rohan reacted instantly. He pulled off his thick cotton jacket, shook it open like a canopy, and held it over both their heads, pulling her along with him as the rain hammered against the concrete steps.

“Run toward the pillar on Platform 3!” he shouted over the roar of the rain.

They sprinted down the slippery stairs, ducking under the rusted iron awning just as the storm reached its peak. They were soaked from the waist down, water dripping from their hair and jackets. But the portfolio tucked between them was bone dry.

Tara leaned against the cold iron pillar, looking down at her drenched shoes, and then suddenly looked up at Rohan. She began to laugh—a loud, relieved laugh that echoed under the tin roof. She reached up and wiped a stream of rainwater from his forehead with her sleeve, shaking her head.

“Look at you, Ro!” she laughed, breathless and happy. “You look like you fell into a well!”

“You don’t look much drier, Tara,” he laughed back, brushing wet hair out of his eyes.

They stood under the awning as the train horns blared in the distance, feeling completely at ease with the storm around them.

Part III — The Hot Chai by the Pillar

It was during that rainstorm on Platform 3, under the slow turn of the rusted ceiling fan, that their routine permanently set itself into stone. An old vendor pushed a tea cart past them, and Rohan bought two glasses of steaming cutting chai, the hot ginger aroma cutting through the damp chill of the platform.

Tara took her glass with both hands, pressing her palms against the warm glass to stop her fingers from shivering. She leaned back against the iron pillar, watching the rain bounce off the tracks, letting out a long, quiet sigh that carried the weight of the last three sleepless nights.

“I’ve never had a friend like you, Rohan,” she said quietly, watching the steam rise. “Before I came to Mumbai, everyone told me architecture was ruthless, that classmates sabotage each other’s models. But with you... I don’t have to keep my guard up.”

Rohan took a sip of his tea, looking out at the grey, rain-swept tracks. He had always been quiet, preferring to stay in the background, uncomfortable with big crowds and loud people. But with Tara, the world made sense. She was honest, stubborn, and completely real.

“You’re good at this, Tara,” Rohan told her honestly. “The clinic drawings today—they had a heart. Most students just draw walls. You draw places where people can feel safe.”

She turned her head to look at him, her dark eyes softening with genuine appreciation. “I only finished it because you fixed the tear, Ro. Don’t pretend you didn’t save my grade.”

“We made a deal, didn’t we? Shared desk, shared tools,” he said.

“Shared everything,” she agreed with a firm nod, finishing the last drop of her tea. In that moment, surrounded by the smell of wet asphalt and iron tracks, their friendship settled into an unbreakable, solid rhythm.

Part IV — The 6:42 Promise

The 6:42 fast local train arrived, its twin yellow headlights cutting through the monsoon dusk. Commuters crowded into the doorways, but Rohan stepped ahead naturally, wedging his shoulder against the iron frame so that Tara could slip through the crowd without being jostled.

They found their spot right by the open doorway, holding the metal handle together as the train accelerated out of Dadar. The cool sea wind blew off Mahim creek, washing over their damp faces. Tara reached into her bag and pulled out a pair of white wired earphones, untangling them with patient fingers.

“Have you ever listened to acoustic music while crossing the creek in the rain?” she asked, holding out the left earpiece.

“Can’t say I have,” Rohan admitted, taking the wire.

“Then put it in, Ro. Don’t talk. Just listen,” she said.

He plugged the small earbud into his ear. A gentle, quiet guitar melody began, drowning out the rattling roar of the steel wheels below them. Outside, the sodium lights of the highway streaked across the dark water like long gold ribbons, blurring in the evening mist.

Sometimes the strongest bond between two people isn’t made of words. It is built in the shared space of a twenty-minute ride, standing side by side against the wind, needing nothing else.

“Promise me we’ll always take this train together, Ro,” she said, her shoulder bumping against his with the sway of the carriage. “Whenever studio gets brutal. Every Friday. Deal?”

Rohan looked out at the dark creek, listening to the guitar notes playing in both their ears, feeling a deep, solid calm settle into his chest.

“Deal,” he answered with a steady smile. “Every Friday. Platform 3, beside the pillar.”

The train banked smoothly toward Matunga. They stood side by side in the open doorway, two tired students sharing a wire, completely comfortable in the quiet trust they had found together, with four long years of blueprints and shared commutes still waiting ahead.

Chapter Thirteen

Monday, Same Seat

“Some promises begin with big words. Others begin with saving someone a seat.”

Part I — The Empty Chair

Monday morning came too soon.

Rohan reached the architecture studio before most of the class. The room still smelled of paper, wood, and the coffee someone had spilled near the back table. He put his bag beside his usual chair and took out his drawing tools.

He looked at the empty chair next to him.

It was a normal chair. A little scratched, one leg shorter than the others. On any other day, he would not have thought about it at all.

Today, he moved his bag from the seat beside him.

“You’re saving that for someone?” a voice asked.

Rohan turned. Tara stood behind him, holding her drawing board against her hip. Her hair was tied up in a messy knot, and she looked as if she had argued with her alarm clock and lost.

“No,” he said. “I’m saving it for the chair’s future.”

Tara stared at him. “Its future?”

“It has plans. Big ones,” he said.

“Does its future include me sitting on it?” Tara asked.

“I’ll have to check with the chair,” he said.

She laughed and dropped into the seat. “Tell it I said thank you.”

Rohan looked down at his pencils so she would not see how happy that small laugh made him.

“You look tired,” he said.

“I am tired.” Tara put her forehead on the desk. “I saw my bed last night and it looked like the most beautiful thing in the world.”

“Did you sleep?” he asked.

“Yes,” she said.

He waited.

“For three hours,” she admitted. “Maybe three and a half if you count the time I was lying down with my eyes closed.”

“That doesn’t count,” he said.

“It counts in my heart,” she said.

“Your heart is not a clock,” he said.

She lifted her head. “You’re very annoying before nine in the morning.”

“And yet you came and sat beside me,” he said.

“The other seats are worse,” she said.

“Of course,” he said.

She took out her pencil case, then stopped and looked at him. “Did you sleep properly?”

“Enough,” he said.

“Rohan,” she said.

“A little less than enough,” Rohan admitted.

“You stayed up late finishing your model too, didn’t you?”

“I was already awake,” he said.

“That is not an answer,” she said.

He shrugged. “I wanted to make sure the edges were clean.”

Tara watched him for a moment. “You know you don’t have to fix everything, right?”

“I don’t fix everything,” he said.

“You fixed my torn plan. You fixed the model base for Nikhil last week. You fixed the studio fan when it started making that horrible noise.”

“The fan was making the horrible noise. I only tightened one screw,” he said.

“And you fixed my mood on Friday,” she said.

He looked up. “I did?”

“You sat there and let me complain for twenty minutes. That counts.”

“I thought you were complaining about the chai,” he said.

“I was complaining about the chai too,” she said.

They both smiled. Then the studio door opened, and their professor walked in carrying a stack of marked sheets.

Tara sat up straight. “Okay. Whatever happens, don’t let me fall asleep during the review.”

“I’ll draw a face on your hand,” he said.

“If you do that, I’ll draw one on yours,” Tara replied.

“Fair,” he said.

Part II — Red Marks

Professor Mehra placed the marked plans on the front table. The class became quiet in the way it always did before marks were announced. Even the students who claimed not to care leaned forward.

“You all worked hard,” he said. “Some of you worked very hard. But effort and result are not always the same thing. Look at the comments. Learn from them.”

He began handing the sheets back.

When Tara got hers, she looked at the red marks first. Her mouth tightened.

Rohan kept his eyes on his own paper, but he could see her tapping the corner of the sheet with her thumb.

“Bad?” he whispered.

“Not bad,” she whispered back. “Just… a lot.”

“What did he write?”

She turned the sheet so he could see. “The layout is clear, but I need to think more about how people actually move through the building. He says my entrance looks nice on paper but would confuse someone using it.”

Rohan studied the drawing. “He’s right about the turn near the waiting area.”

Tara slowly turned to him. “You could have lied to make me feel better.”

“You asked me to look,” he said.

“I did not ask you to destroy me,” she said.

“I’m not destroying you. The turn is just too tight.” He pointed to the plan. “If a person comes in with a bag or a child, they’ll have to stop here. Maybe move this wall back a little.”

She leaned closer. “That would give the waiting area less space.”

“A little. But you could move these seats to the other side.”

Tara followed his pencil with her eyes. “That might work.”

“It’s your design, though. Don’t change it just because I said so.”

“I know.” She looked at the page again. “I’m changing it because it makes sense.”

He nodded, relieved.

A few desks away, Nikhil groaned loudly. “I got a whole paragraph of comments. I think Professor Mehra has written more about my plan than I wrote on it.”

Tara called back, “At least someone read it carefully.”

“I need a friend who supports me,” Nikhil said.

“I support your right to improve,” Tara called back.

“That is the worst kind of support,” Nikhil said.

The class laughed, and the tight feeling in the room eased.

When the professor came past their table, he paused at Tara’s drawing.

“You have a good eye for how a space should feel,” he said. “Now make sure it works as well as it looks.”

Tara nodded. “I will, sir.”

After he moved on, she let out a breath.

“That wasn’t so bad,” he said.

“I didn’t say it was bad,” she said.

“Your face said it was the end of your career,” he said.

“My face is allowed to have private thoughts,” Tara replied.

“Of course.”

She tapped the page. “Can you look at the entrance with me after class? Just ten minutes.”

“Yes,” he said.

“You don’t even know if you’re free,” she said.

“I can be free for ten minutes,” he said.

She gave him a small smile. “Good. Because I want to get it right.”

Part III — A Small Fix

They stayed in the studio after the others went for lunch. Sunlight fell across the drawing tables, bright enough to show every tiny mark on the paper.

Tara moved a ruler along the entrance line. Rohan held the sheet flat while she made a light pencil mark.

“A little more?” she asked.

“Two millimetres,” he said.

“You can see two millimetres?” Tara asked.

“No. I guessed,” Rohan admitted.

She looked at him, then laughed. “You are a terrible assistant.”

“I’m unpaid.”

“You’re getting paid in friendship,” she said.

“That sounds like a bad deal,” he said.

“It is. No refunds,” she said.

They worked quietly for a while. Tara erased the old line and drew a new one. She checked the space, then looked at Rohan.

“Does it feel better?” Tara asked.

He studied it. “Yes. You can see where you’re going now.”

“And the seats?” Rohan asked.

“Try them here,” she said.

She sketched them in, then leaned back. “Okay. That’s better.”

For a moment, neither of them spoke. They were both looking at the same small corner of the drawing, their shoulders almost touching.

Tara broke the silence first. “Do you always know what to do?”

Rohan shook his head. “Not even close.”

“You act like you do.”

“I just take a little longer before I panic,” he said.

She smiled. “That is useful. I panic immediately.”

“I’ve noticed,” he said.

“Hey.” She nudged his arm with her elbow.

“What? It’s true,” he said.

She rolled up the drawing carefully. “Thanks for helping me.”

“You would have figured it out.”

“Maybe. But it would have taken longer.”

He wanted to tell her that he would always make time for her. The thought came so quickly that it frightened him a little.

Instead, he said, “That’s what classmates are for.”

Tara looked at him. “Classmates?”

“And friends,” he added.

“Better.” She held out her fist.

He bumped it with his own. “Better.”

They packed their things and walked towards the canteen. Tara was still talking about the entrance, explaining how she wanted it to feel open without making people feel lost.

Rohan listened. He liked hearing her talk about things she cared about. She used her hands when she spoke, drawing invisible lines in the air. Sometimes she forgot what she was saying and started again. He never minded.

At the stairs, she stopped suddenly.

“Wait. Did you eat anything today?”

“Breakfast,” he said.

“What did you eat?” Tara asked.

“Toast,” he said.

“How much toast?” Tara asked.

“Two pieces,” he said.

She narrowed her eyes. “That is not enough.”

“I’m fine,” he said.

“That is what people say before they faint in front of everyone.”

“I’m not going to faint,” he said.

“Good. Then you can carry my drawing board while I get lunch,” she said.

She handed it to him before he could answer.

Rohan stared at the board in his hands. “You planned this.”

“I planned lunch. The rest is a happy accident.”

Part IV — The Usual Platform

By the end of the day, Tara had made three more changes to her plan and complained about all three. Rohan had listened to every complaint, even the one about the eraser being too small.

At the station, they stood near the familiar pillar while the platform filled with people returning home.

“I think I’m going to sleep for twelve hours,” she said.

“You said that yesterday,” he said.

“Yesterday I was hopeful. Today I have a plan,” she said.

The train announcement crackled overhead. Tara checked the time.

“Not our Friday train,” she said. “Just the normal Monday one.”

“The Friday one is still three days away,” he said.

“I know. I’m counting,” she said.

Rohan smiled. “You’re counting down to a train ride?”

“I’m counting down to a train ride where I don’t have to think about a floor plan.”

“You’ll probably talk about the floor plan anyway,” he said.

“I might. But I’ll be doing it with music,” she said.

The train pulled in. A crowd moved towards the doors, and Rohan stepped aside to let an older woman pass before he and Tara got on.

They found a place near the door, not close enough to be in anyone’s way. Tara held the pole with one hand and her bag with the other.

“Ro?” she said after a while.

“Hmm?” Rohan replied.

“Thanks for saving me a seat today,” she said.

He glanced at her. “You thanked me already.”

“I’m thanking you again,” she said.

“It was just a chair,” he said.

“No. It was a chair next to someone who makes Monday less terrible,” she said.

He looked out at the passing buildings because he did not know what to do with that sentence.

“You make it less terrible too,” he said at last.

Tara smiled, satisfied with the answer. Then she began telling him about a dog she had seen outside the college gate that morning, and the moment passed into something ordinary again.

Rohan was grateful for that. He could keep the words she had said, quietly, without asking them to mean more than they did.

When they reached her stop, Tara stepped down and turned back.

“See you tomorrow, Ro,” she said.

“See you tomorrow,” he said.

She disappeared into the crowd.

Rohan stayed by the door until the train moved again. Monday had been long. The work was not finished, and neither of them had suddenly become different people. But there was a seat beside him that Tara now chose without asking, and a promise waiting for Friday.

For now, that was enough.

Chapter Fourteen

The Space Between Classes

“Sometimes friendship grows in the minutes nobody plans for.”

Part I — The Missing Lunch

Wednesday arrived with a grey sky and a studio full of unfinished work.

Rohan was drawing a set of stairs when Tara dropped into the chair beside him. She put her bag down, opened it, and looked inside.

Then she looked inside again.

“No,” she whispered.

Rohan kept drawing. “What happened?”

“I forgot my lunch,” she said.

“That’s all?” Rohan asked.

She turned to him slowly. “That’s all? Rohan, I have been thinking about the lunch I packed since seven in the morning.”

“What did you pack?” Rohan asked.

“Aloo paratha. Two of them. With pickle,” she said.

“That does sound serious.”

“It is serious. I can still remember the smell.”

He smiled and went back to his drawing.

Tara watched him for a few seconds. “What did you bring?”

“Nothing,” he said.

She blinked. “Nothing?”

“I was going to get something at the canteen,” he said.

“So we are both hungry,” she said.

“Looks like it,” he said.

She folded her arms. “This is a very poor plan from two future architects.”

“We can design a better lunch system,” he said.

“Step one?” Tara asked.

“Find food,” he said.

“Good. Step two?” Tara asked.

“Eat it,” he said.

“Brilliant. You should teach a class,” she said.

They packed their pencils and walked out together. The corridor was busy with students carrying models and sheets of paper. Outside, the air smelled of wet leaves and the rain that had not started yet.

“Canteen?” Rohan asked.

Tara shook her head. “The queue will be too long. Can we go to that Irani place you told me about?”

“The one near the crossing?” Rohan asked.

“Yes. You said the chai is cheap,” she said.

“I said it was five rupees,” he said.

“That is cheap,” she said.

“I didn’t say it was good,” he said.

She stopped walking. “You have been keeping this information from me?”

“I thought you liked surprises,” he said.

“Not when they involve bad chai,” she said.

He laughed. “Come on. We can get something to eat too.”

She hurried to catch up. “If the food is bad, I’m blaming you.”

“That seems fair,” he said.

Part II — Two Plates

The Irani café was small and busy. A ceiling fan turned slowly above the tables, and the glass counter held biscuits, buns, and a few pastries that had been there long enough to look tired.

Tara studied the menu as if it were an important exam.

“What do you usually get?” she asked.

“Bun maska and chai,” he said.

“That’s it?”

“Sometimes an omelette,” he said.

“Today we need something more serious. We have missed lunch.”

“Then order two things,” he said.

She looked at the man behind the counter. “Two bun maska, one omelette, and two chai, please.”

Rohan leaned closer. “You ordered for me?”

“You said that’s what you usually get,” she said.

“I didn’t say I wanted it,” he said.

“Do you want it?” Tara asked.

He looked at the counter. “Yes.”

“Then there’s no problem,” she said.

They found a table near the window. The table wobbled, so Rohan folded a small piece of paper and pushed it under one leg.

Tara watched him. “You really do fix everything.”

“It was wobbling,” he said.

“You could have just held your cup carefully,” she said.

“And let the table live like that?” Rohan asked.

“You’re right. We should call someone,” she said.

“I’ll send it a bill,” he said.

Their food arrived. Tara took one bite of the bun and closed her eyes.

“Okay,” she said. “I forgive the chai in advance.”

“You haven’t tried it yet,” he said.

“The bun has earned my trust,” she said.

They ate for a while, talking about their review sheets, the strange shapes some students had drawn for their models, and a professor who always seemed to appear when someone was trying to hide a mistake.

Tara dipped a piece of bun into her chai and asked, “Can I ask you something?”

“You can ask,” he said.

“Why architecture?” Tara asked.

Rohan thought for a moment. “I like that a building can change how a person feels. A room can make you feel safe. A window can make a small place feel bigger. I think I like the idea of making spaces where people can breathe.”

Tara became quiet, but not in an uncomfortable way.

“That’s a good answer,” she said.

“What about you?”

She turned her cup between her hands. “I want to make homes that don’t make people feel small. My family has moved a few times. Some places were nice, but they never felt like ours. I used to think that if a room had enough light and a corner for your own things, maybe it could feel like home.”

Rohan listened carefully.

“That’s why I care about the small details,” she added. “Where someone puts their shoes. Where a child can sit and draw. Whether a person can open a window without moving a cupboard.”

“I never thought about the cupboard,” he said.

“Most people don’t,” she said.

“I will now,” he said.

She smiled at him over the rim of her cup. “Good. That means I’ve taught you something.”

“One thing. Don’t get too proud,” he said.

“Too late,” she said.

A burst of laughter came from the next table. Outside, the first drops of rain tapped against the glass.

Tara looked towards the street. “I’m glad we came here.”

“Even with the chai?” Rohan asked.

“Especially with the chai. It gives me something to complain about,” she said.

“You never run out of things to complain about,” he said.

“And yet you keep listening,” she said.

He took a sip of tea and tried to sound casual. “Someone has to make sure your complaints are properly recorded.”

Part III — Rain on the Window

The rain grew heavier while they were still at the café. Water ran down the glass in thin lines, and the street outside became a mix of umbrellas, headlights, and people hurrying to find cover.

Tara checked her phone. “We should go back soon.”

“We have time,” he said.

“I know. I just don’t want to be late,” she said.

“We won’t be,” he said.

She put the phone away, then looked at him. “Do you ever get tired of me talking?”

The question came so quietly that he almost thought he had heard it wrong.

“What?” Rohan asked.

“I talk a lot. I know I do. Sometimes I start with one thing and end up telling you about five other things.”

“That’s true,” he said.

She made a face. “You could at least pretend to think about it.”

“I’m thinking.” He put his cup down. “No, I don’t get tired of it.”

“Never?” Tara asked.

“Sometimes I don’t know what you’re talking about,” he said.

“That’s not the same thing,” she said.

“I know.” He paused. “I like hearing what you think about things. Even when it’s five things at once.”

Tara looked down at the table. She moved the paper under the wobbly leg with one finger.

“I don’t always know what I’m thinking until I say it out loud,” she admitted.

“I’ve noticed that too,” he said.

She lifted her head sharply. “You’re supposed to be kind right now.”

“I am being kind,” he said.

“You’re being honest. It’s a different problem,” she said.

He laughed, and she laughed with him. The quiet feeling left the table, but something gentle stayed behind.

A few minutes later, Tara pointed at the rain. “Do you think it will stop before we have to leave?”

“Probably not,” he said.

“You’re supposed to say yes,” she said.

“Why ask me if you want a different answer?” Rohan asked.

“Because I like hope,” she said.

Rohan looked out at the road. “Then yes. It’ll stop soon.”

“Thank you,” she said.

“You’re welcome,” he said.

They waited until the rain became lighter. When they finally stepped outside, Tara held her bag under her jacket and ran ahead two steps before turning back.

“Come on, Ro!” Tara called.

He followed, keeping his pace slow enough that she did not have to wait long.

At the college gate, she shook water from her sleeves.

“That was a good lunch,” she said.

“We should forget lunch more often,” he said.

“Absolutely not. I refuse to make a habit of being hungry,” she said.

“Fair,” he said.

She looked at him for a second. “But we can come back.”

“Whenever you want,” he said.

“Don’t say that. I might take you seriously,” she said.

He smiled. “I know.”

Part IV — A Seat Saved

Back in the studio, the afternoon passed slowly. The rain made the windows dull, and everyone seemed a little quieter. Tara worked on her entrance plan while Rohan finished the stairs he had started that morning.

Every so often, she turned her sheet towards him.

“Does this line look strange?”

He leaned over. “A little.”

“You can just say no,” she said.

“But it does look strange,” he said.

“Fine. I’ll fix it,” she said.

A few minutes later, he asked, “Does this stair landing look too narrow?”

Tara came over and studied his drawing. “A little.”

He looked at her.

She smiled. “I’m allowed to be honest.”

“Apparently,” he said.

They traded comments until both drawings looked better. It was easy to work with her. She did not always agree with him, and she did not expect him to agree with her. Somehow, that made the time feel safe.

When the last class ended, Tara packed her pencils and looked at the chair beside her.

“Tomorrow, I’m sitting here again,” she said.

Rohan raised an eyebrow. “Are you asking permission?”

“No. I’m informing you,” she said.

“Then I’ll inform the chair,” he said.

“Tell it to be ready,” she said.

She swung her bag onto her shoulder, then paused.

“And Ro?” Tara asked.

“Yeah?” Rohan replied.

“Thanks for today. For lunch. And for not making me feel silly when I ask things,” she said.

He wanted to tell her that she never had to worry about being silly around him. He wanted to tell her that he remembered the small things she said because they mattered to him.

Instead, he said, “You make it easy.”

Tara smiled, like that answer was enough. “See you tomorrow.”

“See you,” he said.

She walked out into the corridor, calling goodbye to someone near the stairs. Rohan stayed behind for a moment, looking at the two chairs beside his drawing table.

They were only chairs. The next day would bring more plans, more corrections, more deadlines. Tara might arrive tired or cheerful or annoyed at the weather. He could not know what the next few years would bring, and he did not try to imagine all of it.

He only knew that she had said she would sit beside him tomorrow.

For now, he saved the seat.
