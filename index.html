Chapter One

The first steps of an unspoken love.

The Commute

Every single evening at 6:42, Dadar Station turned into a storm of running feet, loud shouts, and tired faces. Hundreds of people pushed and shoved on Platform 3, just trying to make it home after a long day. But in the middle of all that noise and rushing crowd, Rohan never moved. He always stood under the exact same rusted ceiling fan, waiting by the old iron pillar. His hand held his bag strap tightly, right at the spot where Tara’s headphone wire always used to get caught. He didn’t need to look at the big clock board. He knew her college schedule by heart. He knew which train she caught when she was exhausted, and he knew how many steps she took to walk down the crowded foot overbridge.

"You’re still standing by that same pillar, Ro," a familiar voice said right behind him.

"It’s the only spot where people don’t knock your bag off,"

Rohan replied with an easy, quiet smile, turning around to face her.

"You’re a terrible liar,"

Tara laughed softly. She held up a small paper cup of warm chai.

"You stand here because you know I get lost if you move even five feet away."

She was holding her black drawing tube under one arm. Her hair was loose and slightly messy from the afternoon wind, and her silver earrings swayed gently against her neck. She looked so tired, but the moment she saw him, her shoulders relaxed completely, as if she had finally reached a safe shore. Without saying a word, Rohan reached over and took the heavy plastic drawing tube from her hand, slipping it onto his own shoulder. It was something he had done every day for four years. She never had to ask for help, and he never made her feel like helping was a burden.

The Shield in the Crowd

Far down the dark tracks, the yellow headlights of the local train appeared like two bright eyes. The platform suddenly woke up. People picked up their heavy bags and pushed forward, eager to grab any spot by the train doors. The air smelled of rain, damp clothes, and old railway dust.

"The professor completely tore my project apart today, Ro,"

Tara said, looking down into her half-empty tea. Her voice was unusually quiet.

"He told me my library design was too simple. He said I waste time trying to make buildings feel warm, when all the city cares about is glass and steel."

"He only understands numbers, Tara,"

Rohan told her gently.

"He doesn’t understand that people want to feel safe when they enter a room. They don’t remember how big a building is. They remember how the afternoon sunlight falls across the floor."

"Do you really mean that? Or are you just trying to cheer me up?"

"I’ve seen every single line you’ve drawn since our first semester," he answered, looking straight into her eyes.

"You put your heart into your work. If he can’t feel that, it’s his mistake, not yours."

Tara looked up, and the deep sadness on her face slowly melted away. She didn’t make a joke or look away. She just reached out and gently touched his sleeve with her fingertips. That simple, warm touch meant everything to him, even though he knew she only meant it as gratitude between two close friends. Then, the train came screaming to a halt. The crowd rushed the doors like an angry wave. Rohan moved without thinking. He stepped in front of her, wedging his broad left shoulder hard against the steel doorframe. He made his body into a wall, taking all the hard shoves and elbows from the crowd so not a single person would push into her.

"Grab my jacket and step in," he called over the screech of the brakes.

"I’m holding on, Ro," she whispered, her fingers clutching his jacket with total, unquestioned trust. He guided her inside the compartment safely, straight to the open doorway where the cool evening breeze could touch their faces and give them room to breathe.

The Shared Earphone

As the train picked up speed past Matunga, the heavy, humid air of the platform turned into cool, fresh wind. Down below the railway bridges, the dark water of Mahim creek rushed past, catching the orange city streetlights like spilled paint. Everyone around them was quiet and exhausted, resting against the metal walls as the train swayed back and forth on the tracks. Tara reached into her small pocket and pulled out her white wired earphones. She untangled them patiently, put the right one into her own ear, and handed the other side to Rohan. She didn’t even have to ask. Sharing this wire was a habit they had kept on this same train for hundreds of evenings.

"True care doesn’t need big promises. It lives inside simple habits—sharing an earphone, standing beside someone in the crowd, and staying close through the dark ride home."

A gentle acoustic song filled the earbud, drowning out the loud metal noise of the train wheels. Rohan leaned his shoulder against the doorframe, watching the cool sea breeze move the loose hair around her forehead. She leaned against the pillar right next to him, her arm resting gently against his jacket.

"Do you ever wonder what happens after college, Ro?" she asked suddenly, her voice quiet against the wind.

"What do you mean?" he asked, turning his head slightly toward her.

"When we become real architects. When we have our own separate jobs, and we don’t take the 6:42 local together anymore. What if we get too busy for this?"

"I don’t think either of us will ever forget this train, Tara," he murmured.

"Promise me something, then," she said, looking right at him with deep, serious eyes.

"Promise that no matter how big life gets, we won’t become those strangers who only message each other once a year on birthdays."

Her words felt like a quiet ache in his chest. She was asking for an eternity of friendship, completely unaware that he loved her with every piece of his heart. But he didn’t let his eyes waver. He swallowed his unspoken pain, smiled softly, and gave her the only answer that mattered.

"I promise," he whispered into the rush of wind.

"I’m never going anywhere."
